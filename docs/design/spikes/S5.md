# Spike S5: chunked launches

- **Date:** 2026-11-25
- **Machine:** Xenon (WSL2, RTX 3090 Ti, compute capability 8.6, i9-12900K; the desktop and local
  LLM inference share the card)
- **Backlog:** S5 ([#11](https://github.com/eoin-obrien/brotkit/issues/11))
- **Code:** [`spikes/S5_chunks/`](../../../spikes/S5_chunks/) (throwaway). Library code that came
  out of it: `brotkit.backends.chunks` (the chunk planner, GPU-free), tested in
  `tests/test_chunks.py` against a simulated GPU built from this spike's measurements.
- **Outcome:** all three gates **pass**, with the overhead gate in the form the maintainer
  revised after the first sweep and a narrow, documented exclusion for clock warm-up
  (ADR-0009; removed by iteration-sliced launches, #76). No chunk came near the hard ceiling
  (longest 0.31 s against 0.5 s), and Ctrl-C in a real Jupyter kernel stops within the chunk in
  flight with a correct partial result. With the time model stored in the kernel cache,
  renders under 3.7 s cost 4.6–32.9 ms of overhead and longer ones 0.15–0.97%, cold and warm.
  Recorded as [ADR-0009](../decisions.md).

## Summary

| --- | --- |
| **WDDM watchdog under WSL2** | No `Tdr*` value is set under `HKLM\System\CurrentControlSet\Control\GraphicsDrivers`, so the Windows defaults apply: `TdrDelay` 2 s, `TdrLevel` 3 (recover). brotkit's hard ceiling on a chunk is 0.5 s, a quarter of that. ## The watchdog and the ceiling

Under WSL2 the GPU belongs to Windows, and WDDM's Timeout Detection and Recovery applies to
every CUDA launch: a GPU job that doesn't yield within `TdrDelay` resets the driver, which kills
the CUDA context (every brotkit array and kernel in the process) and, with `TdrLevel` 3, blanks
the desktop for a moment. None of the `Tdr*` values is set on Xenon, so `TdrDelay` is the 2 s
default.

- **Hard ceiling: 0.5 s** (`DEFAULT_CEILING_S`, a quarter of `TdrDelay`), separate from the
  100 ms target. The planner issues no chunk whose worst case (with a 1.25× safety margin) is
  above it.
- **A running kernel can't be cancelled.** CUDA has no way to stop a launched kernel short of
  destroying the context. So the ceiling is also the worst-case cancel latency: after Ctrl-C,
  brotkit waits for the chunk in flight, at most 0.5 s.
- **Long single launches** (`tdr.py`): one dd band of all-interior pixels, sized to 0.25, 0.5,
  1.0 and 1.5 s from launches on the same rows run just before it, never past 75% of
  `TdrDelay`. They took 0.15, 0.22, 0.74 and 1.33 s, completed, and a render in the same
  context worked after each. The test stopped there by design; it says nothing about longer
  launches.

## Time model

`curve.py` timed tiles of all-interior pixels (every pixel at max_iter 2000) from 256 to 8.3
million pixels, at boost clock after a warm-up, per tier:

| Tier | τ per iteration | R (Giter/s) | Knee `R·τ` (px) | max_iter at the 0.5 s ceiling (÷1.25) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| f32 | ~75 ns | ~620 | ~46,000 | ~5.3 million |
| df64 | ~265 ns | ~97 | ~26,000 | ~1.5 million |
| f64 | ~1.26 µs | ~18 | ~23,000 | ~320,000 |
| dd | ~14.7 µs | ~1.47 | ~22,000 | **~27,000** |
| qf | ~1.6 µs | ~14.8 | ~24,000 | ~250,000 |

- Below the knee the time is flat at about `m·τ`. The additive model `m·τ + p·m/R` bounds every
  measured point; `max(m·τ, p·m/R)` underestimates by up to 1.6× near the knee.
- **τ is per launch block, not per thread.** A (32, 8) block's 8 warps share an SM's few FP64
  units, so a lone warp at max_iter in an otherwise finished block ran about 7× faster than
  `τ` in dd (an 8×32 chunk with one interior pixel took 22 ms instead of 0.145 s). Only chunks
  whose every pixel reaches the largest count, with at least one full block, measure `τ`.
- **NaN probes.** NaN coordinates make every pixel run to max_iter whatever the formula
  (`r2 > BAIL` is false for NaN), which gives the planner an all-interior tile on demand. NaN τ
  against real interior τ (`nan_tau.py`, 8×1024 tiles): 0.94–1.00 for kernel C and 1.00–1.66 for
  F6 (libdevice's `atan2`/`sin` take slower paths on NaN), so a NaN probe never reads low by more
  than the 1.25 safety margin covers. Calibration probes write to a scratch buffer, never the
  image.
- **The clock.** The GPU idles at 210 MHz and drops there between renders. The first launches
  of a render ran 3–9× slower than warm ones (dd: τ 126 µs, then 41, 18 and 14.5 µs as it
  warmed), and in dd small chunks kept it from reaching full speed for tens of seconds. Every
  estimate therefore has to keep moving after the first probe.

## The planner (`brotkit.backends.chunks`)

GPU-free: the caller launches, times each chunk with events, reads back its iteration sum and
largest escape count, and calls `record`. The API is tile-shaped (`Tile(row0, col0, th, tw)`);
bands are full-width tiles, and `ChunkPolicy(tile=N)` carves N×N tiles instead.

1. **Calibration** on NaN coordinates in scratch. A latency probe (8×128 px) grows its
   iteration count 4× from 64 while its projected cost stays under 20 ms; then a throughput
   probe (64×4096 px) runs at the largest count the budget allows, repeated until `R` improves
   by less than 10% (at most 8 probes).
2. **Learning from every chunk.** `R` is the best measured iterations per second; `τ` is the
   least `seconds / largest count` over uniform chunks. Both can only err slow, so they tighten
   as the GPU warms. Two more latency probes run after 0.5 s and 2 s of GPU time, because large
   chunks are throughput-bound and never show `τ`. When a chunk runs in under 0.65× of the
   model's time for its own iterations while the ceiling limits growth, `R` is stale and the
   planner inserts another throughput probe (at most 8, stopping once one doesn't improve `R`).
   A chunk that runs over its bound scales `R` and `τ` back by the overrun, so the planner
   follows a GPU that slows down (a second process, a lower clock).
3. **Sizing.** Each chunk takes the most pixels whose worst case stays under the ceiling, never
   below the knee while the ceiling allows, aiming for `max(target, floor / 0.25)` from the
   recent chunks' cost (the floor costs at most a quarter of a chunk). Bands are whole 8-row
   strips, rounded up when that stays under the ceiling; a single strip too big for the budget
   is cut into column segments.
4. **Infeasible renders.** If `max_iter·τ` alone breaks the ceiling, no spatial chunk can. The
   planner runs up to 3 confirming latency probes (each held to half the ceiling, warming the
   clock) and then raises `CeilingInfeasibleError`, naming the largest feasible max_iter.
5. **Stored prior** (after the first sweep). `prior=` takes `Estimates(tau, rate)` from the
   kernel cache's perf record for this kernel on this device, written back after every render.
   With a prior there are no latency or throughput calibration passes. The model is the prior
   slowed by two factors, `tau = prior.tau × c_tau` and `R = prior.rate ÷ c_rate`, or by what
   the render has measured where that is better. Both factors start at 10 (S5 measured 3–9×
   after idle). The first chunk is a real one, sized so its worst case at that slowdown fits
   the 100 ms target, and it stays under the ceiling even with a prior up to 50× too fast.
   After each chunk the prior puts at 1 ms or more, `seconds ÷ prior model` updates `c_tau`,
   and updates `c_rate` too if the prior's throughput term dominates. Each factor is the
   largest ratio over its last two chunks, never below 1.
   - **Why two factors.** A short render learns `τ` only as a loose upper bound (df64:
     220× high, from throughput-bound chunks). In one sweep run a single factor took a latency
     probe that beat that `τ` as "warm" and applied the prior's full `R` at 375 MHz: a chunk
     ran at 1.88× its bound (0.19 s).
   - **The floor-heavy trap.** At `c_rate` = 10, dd's chunks are all latency-bound, so none
     can lower `c_rate`; dd/whole ran 794 chunks in 59 s. So when the last two real chunks
     were latency-bound and the ceiling, not the target, sizes chunks, one small throughput
     probe in scratch (2 ms of the prior's throughput time) measures `R`'s slowdown. It is timed against the prior's throughput term alone,
     runs at most once per 4 chunks, and counts as calibration.
   - **Probes.** A NaN latency probe runs first only when the floor at the current `c_tau`
     breaks the ceiling (dd at 10,000 iterations on a cold clock), followed by the confirming
     probes. A kernel with no record yet calibrates as in step 1.
   - **Safety of the record.** Each device's record carries a sha256 of its JSON, and the file
     is replaced atomically. A damaged, half-written or lost record reads as none, and the
     render starts from the tiny probe, so damage can only make the first launch smaller.
     Tests cover concurrent writers in two processes, a changed digit, and a prior that errs
     slow (it never gives a larger first chunk).

The first version of the planner trusted cheap probes and fell into a trap: chunks below the
knee only ever show a low `R`, so they stayed small. dd/interior ran 6,181 chunks in 458 s
against about 80 s now. The bugs and fixes in order (each with a regression test in
`tests/test_chunks.py`): rate measured on exterior and sub-knee chunks (throughput probe); the
latency estimate taken from exterior pixels (NaN probes); band rows rounded down below the knee
(round up); the scratch buffer sized for the latency probe only (`scratch_shape`; this crashed
the first sweep with `CUDA_ERROR_ILLEGAL_ADDRESS`); a cold clock refusing a feasible dd render
(confirming probes); `τ` taken from a lone warp (uniform chunks only); a cold `R` keeping dd in
1,621 chunks for 243 s (repeated and late throughput probes, floor share); a cold `τ` sizing
f64 chunks at 240 ms (late latency probes); and the warm-start overrun (cold factor).

The simulated GPU in the tests follows these measurements (latency floor, throughput with warp
divergence, the knee penalty, FP64 blocks, cold clocks, sudden slowdowns); the hardware sweep
below is the real check.

## Clock warm-up: the one exclusion from planner overhead

After the sweep above, the maintainer chose to report clock warm-up apart from planner
overhead, with the exception kept narrow:

- **Definition.** A launch is *clock warm-up* when its only purpose is to raise the clock, and
  it was issued because no real chunk, not even one thread × the full `max_iter`, fits under
  the ceiling at the current, measured clock. The planner flags it `Chunk.warmup`. In practice
  these are the confirming probes a render with a prior runs after its first probe has
  measured the clock: dd at 10,000 iterations started from an idle GPU.
- **Everything else counts as overhead:** the first probe (it measures the clock), every
  probe in a render without a prior (they also measure `τ`), and all other calibration.
- **Still visible.** Warm-up time is reported separately in the render's timing
  (`ChunkPlanner.warmup_seconds`; the runner's `warmup_s` and `warmup_chunks`).
- **Tested** (`tests/test_chunks.py`): a warm-up chunk is only ever issued with a prior, after
  the clock was measured, and when the floor breaks the ceiling (a property test over tiers,
  iteration counts and cold factors). A warm GPU, a shallow render, and the f32, f64 and qf
  tiers at 10,000 iterations on a 9× cold clock never trigger one. Without a prior, probes
  are never warm-up.
- **Temporary.** Iteration-sliced launches
  ([#76](https://github.com/eoin-obrien/brotkit/issues/76), backlog 1.29) let real chunks run
  while the clock rises. That change drops this exclusion and re-runs the gate.

## The clock ramp and the warm-up kernel

`warmup.py`: wait for the idle clock, launch a warm-up kernel (one warp per SM, a dependent FMA
chain, on a second stream) for H ms, sleep H ms standing in for the host's render setup, then
time 300 back-to-back qf latency probes (about 0.9 ms each when warm). 3 repetitions:

| Head start H | First probe | GPU time until under 1.2× warm | Cold penalty (mean) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 8.6× | 308–327 ms | 282 ms |
| 5 ms | 8.6–13× | 217–323 ms | 255 ms |
| 20 ms | 8.7–8.8× | 234–324 ms | 260 ms |
| 50 ms | 8.7–10.2× | 134–312 ms | 207 ms |
| 100 ms | 8.7–10.2× | 75–259 ms | 155 ms |
| 200 ms | 1.05–4.7× | 1–152 ms | 50 ms |

- The clock (210 MHz idle, 1950 MHz under load) doesn't rise gradually. It stays low for about
  100–300 ms of continuous load, then jumps, so the cold penalty is about **280 ms of extra GPU
  time** for a render that starts from idle. It isn't counted as overhead (it is kernel time),
  but a cold render is that much slower than a warm one.
- **A warm-up kernel removes only what it overlaps.** A head start of H ms removes about H ms
  of penalty, more or less. A render's real host setup is short: 0.08 ms for a memory-hit
  lookup (0.5 ms disk hit), about 10 ms to generate the kernel source in the spike's
  generator, and 5 ms to allocate 4K layer buffers. So a warm-up launched at the start of a
  render hides about 15 ms of the 280 ms penalty, about 5%. The 4K sweep above ran without
  one.
- **Where it pays: a kernel build or disk load.** The runner queues about 300 ms of warm
  kernel (sized at the idle clock, so it ends soon after the clock jumps) on a side stream
  just before a lookup that misses memory, and never on a memory hit. `warm_build.py` timed
  qf/whole on an idle GPU, with the qf kernel built from an empty cache (2.5–4.8 s), 2 reps:

  | Warm-up during the build | Build | Render GPU time (chunks + calibration) |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | none | 2.99 s, 4.78 s | 2.65 s, 2.72 s |
  | one queued batch (3–5 ms to queue) | 3.04 s, 2.70 s | 2.59 s, 2.55 s |
  | a helper thread polling every 0.5 ms (first version) | 2.86 s, 3.25 s against 2.48 s, 2.90 s without | 2.47 s, 2.42 s against 2.65 s, 2.70 s without |

  The batch saves 63–167 ms of the render's GPU time and doesn't measurably slow the build
  (build times vary by ±1 s). The thread saved more (180–280 ms) but slowed both builds by
  0.36 s, a net loss, so it was dropped.

## Pipelining (not done)

Keeping two chunks in flight would hide the latency floor at each chunk's tail, where a few
slow warps hold the GPU, and the 0.3–0.6 ms of host time between chunks. The cost is a
worst-case cancel latency of up to two chunks (two ceilings, 1 s), unless the kernel checks a
host-mapped cancel flag on entry. Backlog 1.30; not in this PR.

## Waiting for a chunk

On WSL2 a blocking-sync wait does not block. With `EventOptions(blocking_sync=True)`, and even
with the context flag set by `cuCtxSetFlags(CU_CTX_SCHED_BLOCKING_SYNC)` (flags read back as
`BLOCKING_SYNC | MAP_HOST`), `Event.sync()` and `Stream.sync()` used a full core for the whole
wait, the same as spinning. A helper thread doing the blocking wait spins too. Only polling
`Event.is_done` (a property in cuda.core) with sleeps frees the CPU: 1.2% of a core at a 1 ms
interval over a 0.3 s kernel.

brotkit's runner uses **hybrid**: sleep in 1 ms slices until half the expected chunk time, then
poll every 0.1 ms. Across the sweep it used 7–12% of a core on renders over 0.2 s (planning
and readback included), against 100% for spin. Per-chunk fixed cost on 200 tiny chunks
(`fixed_cost.py`):

| Wait | Wall per chunk | GPU: kernel / tile_sum | Host | CPU / wall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| spin | 205 µs | 65 / 45 µs | 95 µs | 95% |
| block | 189 µs | 48 / 36 µs | 106 µs | 100% |
| thread | 616 µs | 102 / 60 µs | 453 µs | 86% |
| poll 2ms | 2681 µs | 171 / 74 µs | 2437 µs | 23% |
| poll 0.5ms | 964 µs | 118 / 58 µs | 789 µs | 42% |
| poll 0.1ms | 484 µs | 89 / 55 µs | 340 µs | 64% |
| hybrid 0.1ms | 510 µs | 97 / 60 µs | 352 µs | 67% |

These chunks do almost no work, so the CPU column is mostly the host's own per-chunk work, not
waiting. "thread" is a helper thread doing the blocking-sync wait, which spins anyway. Polling
at 0.5–2 ms adds up to 2.4 ms of oversleep per chunk; hybrid wakes within about 0.1 ms of the
end once past half the expected time.

On a real chunk the host side is about 0.6 ms: two launches (0.12 ms each), the 16-byte
readback (0.25 ms) and the planner (0.05 ms). Hiding it would take the next chunk queued before
the current one finishes, which would double the cancel latency unless the kernel checks a
cancel flag on entry (backlog 1.30).

## Jupyter

`jupyter_interrupt.py` starts an ipykernel with `jupyter_client`, runs a long dd render
(interior, max_iter 5000, about 33 s) in a cell, and after 3–6 s calls `interrupt_kernel()`,
which sends SIGINT as JupyterLab's stop button does.

The runner defers SIGINT: the first one sets a flag that the loop checks between chunks, so the
chunk in flight is always finished and recorded and no pixel is written without being flagged
done. A second SIGINT raises at once (the result is still flagged incomplete). The render raises
`RenderInterrupted`, a `KeyboardInterrupt` that carries the partial result.

| Wait | Rounds | Interrupt to idle kernel | Last chunk | Partial result | Re-run |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| spin | 3 | 0.26–0.41 s | 0.22–0.36 s | complete False; 9–19% done; done tiles bit-identical to a full render; the rest untouched (sentinel); no part-written tile | complete, 0 new kernel lookups |
| hybrid | 3 | 0.13–0.30 s | 0.22–0.36 s | same | same |

CPU during the render: 100% of a core with spin, 8% with hybrid.

### Manual checklist (real JupyterLab)

Run once from `spikes/S5_chunks`: `uv run --with jupyterlab jupyter lab manual_check.ipynb`,
with the spike venv's kernel.

- [ ] **Progress:** the qf render's progress line updates in place, about ten times a second,
      and reaches 100%.
- [ ] **Interrupt:** the stop button stops the dd render within the chunk in flight (about
      0.3 s), with `RenderInterrupted`; the progress line freezes below 100%.
- [ ] **Partial result:** the next cell prints `complete False`, a done fraction below 1, and
      `True True`.
- [ ] **Re-run after the interrupt:** the f32 render finishes with `complete True` and the
      kernel lookup count unchanged (3).

## Recommendations and design-doc changes

- **Adopt the planner** (ADR-0009) with the stored prior. It passes the ceiling, cancel and
  revised overhead gates, with clock warm-up reported apart until iteration-sliced launches
  (#76) remove the need for it. Settings: hard ceiling 0.5 s, target 100 ms, worst case planned
  for every chunk, estimates from the stored prior and from every chunk, NaN calibration in
  scratch only without a prior (plus the few cheap probes above). The spec's "50–200 ms,
  measured on the first chunk" changes accordingly.
- **Warm-up kernel only around a kernel build or disk load**, not on every render.
- **A dd refusal names qf**, which goes about 10× deeper in one launch at about 96 against
  106 mantissa bits, and backlog 1.29 (#76).
- **Bands by default; tiles at 512 px** when tiling needs them. The spec's 256–512 px tiles as
  interrupt chunks change (256 px costs up to 27%).
- **Hybrid wait and deferred SIGINT** in the CUDA runner (backlog 1.4).
- **Iteration-sliced launches** (backlog 1.29): resumable kernels that carry each pixel's state
  across launches, needed for dd past about 27,000 iterations and for any tier whose
  `max_iter·τ` breaks the ceiling (deep zooms in Phase 4 included). Until then the planner
  refuses those renders with `CeilingInfeasibleError`.
- **Pipelined launches with a cancel flag** (backlog 1.30), if the remaining 0.3–1 ms per chunk
  matters: the next chunk queued before the current one ends, and the kernel returning at once
  when a host-mapped flag is set, so the cancel latency stays one chunk.
- **`doctor` reports `TdrDelay` and `TdrLevel`** under WSL2 (backlog 1.20), and warns if
  `TdrDelay` is below 2 s, since the ceiling assumes it.
